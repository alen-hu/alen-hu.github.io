Usage Practices and Trust in Artificial Intelligence: An Analytical Sociology Approach

Publication / Independent Authored Research Study (2025 - 2026)
Manuscript under peer review Preprint (ResearchGate) Replication Code (R / GitHub)

This study investigates the micro-to-macro mechanisms driving the diffusion of Large Language Models (LLMs) and trust formation among university students by rigorously combining empirical survey data with agent-based modelling implemented in R. Grounded in Hedström’s DBO (Desires, Beliefs, Opportunities) theory, the research constructs a custom 7-section questionnaire administered to a non-probabilistic sample of Croatian university students (N=309) to capture usage practices, motivational drivers, perceived norms, and evolving trust structures.

Methodologically, a simulation model was developed over 100 timesteps and initialized with empirical survey data to bridge individual micro-actions with macro-level adoption outcomes. The model incorporates a homophily-based network topology, formalizing individual social thresholds, expectation-correction mechanisms, and dynamic trust-belief feedback loops. Exploratory factor analysis identified latent theoretical constructs, which were subsequently analyzed using logistic and OLS regression models alongside longitudinal mixed-effects modelling.

The research demonstrates that rational imitation within peer networks serves as the primary predictor of technology adoption in early stages, whereas the actual quality and depth of usage practices critically govern the longitudinal development of trust. By isolating the distinct effects of rational imitation versus normative pressure, the study offers a robust mechanism-based explanation for technology diffusion, contributing directly to computational social sciences and the sociological understanding of human-AI interaction.

The Concept of Interaction in Sociology

Master’s Thesis Research • Faculty of Croatian Studies, University of Zagreb (2025 - 2026)
Master’s Thesis (FHS Repository)

This master's thesis represents a systematic conceptual analysis of how interaction is theorized across major sociological traditions. Rather than treating interaction as a self-evident background category, the research examines it simultaneously as a situational process unfolding between actors, a relational pattern capable of stabilizing social relations, and a core micro-foundational mechanism mediating between individual action and broader macro-social outcomes.

Special attention is dedicated to how classical and contemporary paradigms - including systems theory and analytical sociology - operationalize interaction in empirical research. The thesis directly addresses the intricate relationship between conceptual clarity and methodological tractability, tackling the enduring sociological problem of bridging micro-social interactive processes with macro-social structural phenomena.

The study develops a comprehensive theoretical synthesis of existing approaches, establishing a rigorous conceptual framework designed to analyze the structural coupling between individual-level behavioral dynamics and large-scale social patterns. Under the supervision of Assistant Professor Erik Brezovec, PhD, the thesis bridges foundational sociological theory with contemporary analytical demands.
